The Hidden Tax: What Food Delivery Commissions Are Really Taking From You

25 to 35 percent. On a ₹400 order, that's ₹100–140 gone before packaging, prep, and rent. The math most operators never do — and what it means to own your ordering channel.

There's a number most café owners know but rarely say out loud: 25 to 35 percent. That's the commission range on most major food delivery platforms operating in India. On a ₹400 order, you're handing over ₹100 to ₹140 before accounting for packaging, the time your kitchen spends on delivery prep, and the real estate your counter gives up to delivery bags that sit there waiting.

On paper, this feels manageable. You're getting orders you wouldn't otherwise have. The platform handles discovery, the customer experience, and the logistics. In practice, the math tells a very different story.

The real margin math

Well-run cafés in India typically operate on food margins of 20 to 35 percent — the difference between the selling price and the direct cost of making the item. When a delivery platform takes 28 percent of the order value, and your food cost is another 32 percent, and rent and staff are fixed costs that don't scale down for delivery orders, you are making very little — sometimes nothing — on every order that comes through the platform.

The workaround most operators land on: hiking delivery menu prices by 20 to 25 percent to compensate. But this creates a different problem entirely.

Customers see the price gap. They order your ₹280 cold brew on the platform and find it for ₹220 when they walk in. The trust erodes. Your brand, built on quality and value, starts to look inconsistent. For an independent café that competes on character and experience, this is a slow, quiet form of brand damage.

What you're actually trading

Delivery platforms don't just take a commission. They own the customer relationship. The person who orders from you three times a week through an aggregator doesn't know your café's phone number. They've never seen your Instagram. They will see a competitor's sponsored listing the moment they open the app, because every interaction with your café happens inside someone else's product.

You are renting attention from a platform that will always put its business interests before yours. When the platform changes its algorithm, your visibility changes. When it decides to promote its own cloud kitchen brands, you're competing with the distribution network you're paying to use.

The customer who orders through an aggregator knows the platform. The customer who orders directly knows you.

The case for owned ordering

Owning your ordering channel doesn't mean abandoning delivery platforms entirely. It means building a direct ordering option — QR-based, link-based, or app-based — and actively moving customers toward it over time.

When a customer orders directly from you, you know who they are. You know what they ordered, how often they order, and when they last visited. You can build a loyalty program around that. You can send a message when you launch a new menu item. You can bring back a lapsed customer with a quiet nudge that costs nothing.

Cafés that have shifted a meaningful portion of their orders to self-managed digital ordering consistently report two things: better margins and stronger repeat purchase rates. Because the customer who orders directly isn't choosing convenience — they're choosing you.

A realistic transition

The shift to owned ordering is gradual, not sudden. Start by giving your dine-in guests a reason to order directly next time — a loyalty point, a smaller menu that rewards direct customers, a QR code on the receipt that leads to your own ordering page. Every direct customer you acquire reduces your dependence on platforms that take a third of your revenue for the privilege.

The goal isn't to replace the platform. It's to make sure the platform is a discovery tool, not your primary sales channel. Discovery is worth paying for. Operations are not.
